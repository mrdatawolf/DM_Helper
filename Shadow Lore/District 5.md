# District 5

## Shadow Overview

District 5 is a modern American shadow in which the United States still possesses its land, population, industry, and accumulated technical knowledge, but has lost the institutional ability to use them well. The country has fallen from its former position as a global power. It can no longer reliably project force abroad or promise progress at home, and its government has answered decline by turning inward. What it can no longer inspire through success, it demands through obedience.

Most of the country still looks familiar. People work, attend school, follow sports, repair their homes, argue about money, and expect their children to grow up. Elections, courts, agencies, and familiar civic language may still exist, but their exact condition has not yet been fixed. The important truth is that government failed before resources or population did. Its remaining institutions have become better at testing loyalty than solving problems.

Technology is recognizably present-day, but new equipment has been difficult to source for almost a decade. Communities keep old systems alive because they can be repaired locally. Analog radios, printed maps, wired cameras, refurbished vehicles, obsolete computers, and scavenged components have returned to ordinary use. The state suffers from the same shortages, giving its surveillance and broadcasts a patched-together quality without making them any less oppressive.

The shadow takes its name from an island in the Pacific Northwest now designated **District 5**. The island once held an Indigenous community and a small town, with homes and civic buildings scattered through its forests. The government has erased the island's earlier name from modern maps and converted the abandoned settlement into a permanent killing ground. What happened to the people who lived there is not initially known. Their presence survives only in fragments the state has painted over, sealed away, or repurposed.

**Shadow stats:** Order 94 / Chaos 6 / Dream 0. Pattern influence: Pattern.

## The National Surrender Program

Every region of the country must surrender a fixed number of recent high-school graduates to the state. A new game is held each month, year-round. Each cohort contains roughly thirty students drawn from the same regional community, often the same school and graduating class.

That familiarity is essential. The state does not want anonymous competitors. It wants friends, rivals, teammates, former partners, relatives, and children who grew up together. Its idea of sacrifice requires the contestants to watch the people they love and know be ground down for the benefit of the state. The eventual survivor must live because everyone around them did not.

Children are never told that the National Surrender Program exists. There is no elaborate false future promised to them: they simply believe that graduation means the beginning of adult life. For the vast majority, it does. They leave home for jobs, college, relationships, military service, or whatever comes next. The secrecy works because selection is possible rather than universal.

The chosen graduates are drugged during what appears to be ordinary transit. They awaken at school desks in a makeshift classroom on District 5. The windows are covered, their heads are still heavy from the drugs, and every person around them is someone they recognize. A government official is physically present to welcome them and explain their new civic duty.

The official is courteous, patient, and practiced. They know the students' names and speak as though administering a difficult but honorable graduation requirement. Anger is treated as childishness. Pleas for mercy receive regretful explanations about equality and duty. The official's calm makes clear that the students' objections have been heard many times before and have never mattered.

The Program's preferred maxim is:

> **What we surrender together, we survive together.**

## The Rules of a Surrender

Each student receives a locked explosive collar, a map, basic food and water, and one randomly assigned supply bag. A bag might contain an effective weapon, a useful tool, or nearly worthless household goods. The lottery is held up as proof of fairness: every student has been subjected to the same procedure, so the state denies responsibility for the unequal results.

The island is divided into numbered zones. Regular broadcasts announce the dead and designate new forbidden zones. Entering a forbidden zone activates the student's collar. The changing map prevents the class from hiding until the deadline, separates allies, and steadily forces the survivors toward one another.

The cohort has three days. Only one student may leave District 5 alive. If more than one collar remains active at the deadline, every remaining collar detonates.

There are no monsters, altered animals, or supernatural hazards on the island. The threat is entirely human: fear, hunger, resentment, betrayal, obedience, and the decisions people make when compassion has been given a deadline. Even the island's dangers are operated by people in control rooms. District 5 is crueler for needing no monster other than its institutions and the citizens who sustain them.

## The Collars

The collar is the perfect symbol of the Program. It tracks, listens, threatens, and reduces a student to a signal on an operator's screen. The state controls the contestants' bodies while insisting their choices remain free. Officials claim they never order one student to murder another. They merely establish the rules, distribute the supplies, close the zones, and enforce the deadline.

In this way, the government turns coercion into personal guilt. Every killing can be described as an individual moral failure, while the machinery that made it inevitable remains blameless and necessary.

The collar also marks the exact moment childhood ends. Adults have spent eighteen years protecting the student from knowledge of the Program. The state replaces that protection with a device around the throat and calls the change maturity.

## The Adults Who Watch

Adults know the Program exists. They know they are expected to watch each monthly Surrender, and they know children must never learn about it. Discussion around minors is self-censored so completely that even indirect questions can make a room fall silent. An adult who speaks openly risks being attacked by neighbors, reported to authorities, or blamed for burdening children with knowledge they are supposedly too young to carry.

Watching is presented as solemn civic duty rather than entertainment. Refusal is treated as cowardice: an unwillingness to witness the sacrifice that preserves everyone else's way of life. Employers organize viewings. Neighborhoods notice dark screens and absent faces. Adults shame other adults who turn away, and the broadcast doubles as a quiet means of identifying people whose loyalty has weakened.

The parents of graduating students know their children could be selected, but they do not know in advance whether they will be. They pack bags, take graduation photographs, offer advice, and say goodbye while preserving the lie. Most tell themselves that warning their child would only fill an otherwise normal transition with needless terror. Once selection occurs, that kindness is revealed as complicity.

Not every adult believes the doctrine in the same way. Some watch from fear. Some have convinced themselves that witnessing is a form of respect. Some believe the Program prevents civil war or proves no region is above another. Some are grateful whenever another family's child is selected. Others have repeated the public language so long that they can no longer locate the point where performance became belief.

This is why the system endures. The government does not require universal enthusiasm. It requires silence around children, visible participation among adults, and enough frightened people to punish anyone who breaks either rule.

## The Broadcast and the Survivor

The broadcast shows real events, but truth is arranged into propaganda. Editors determine context. Commentators interpret cooperation as weakness, terror as cowardice, killing as acceptance of responsibility, and obedience as dignity. The government does not need to invent what happened when it controls what every action is permitted to mean.

Each game produces one official survivor. Among adults, survivors are honored as **Exemplars**: citizens who accepted the nation's burden and proved worthy of its investment. Among children, they are simply successful graduates who received scholarships, government placements, or prestigious assignments elsewhere.

An Exemplar cannot return to an ordinary life without threatening the secrecy that protects the Program. Survivors are rewarded materially but absorbed permanently into the adult machinery. They may become commentators, recruiters, security personnel, public officials, or silent symbols displayed when the state requires them. Winning preserves life, but not necessarily ownership of that life.

## The Dark Mirror

District 5 is a dark reflection of a familiar modern world, not an alien dystopia. Its people possess recognizable virtues: they love their families, value fairness, endure hardship, take pride in community, and want their country to survive. The state has not eliminated those virtues. It has taught people to express them through cruelty.

Parental love becomes a reason to preserve ignorance. Fairness becomes equal exposure to arbitrary death. Shared responsibility becomes universal complicity. Civic participation becomes compulsory spectatorship. Sacrifice ceases to mean choosing what one will give and instead means accepting the state's right to take from somebody else.

The Program is possible because most daily life remains ordinary. Citizens can condemn it privately and still go to work the next morning. They can love their own children while watching another person's child die. They can believe themselves decent because they did not design the system, then defend it because admitting its evil would force them to confront everything their silence has purchased.

District 5 asks how far normal people can be led when each surrender is small, shared, procedurally equal, and described as the price of preventing something worse.

## The Hidden Order

Nothing within District 5 appears magical. Its people know nothing of Amber, the Pattern, the Logrus, the Argent Refrain, or Shadow. Its horror should remain entirely mundane from the player's perspective.

Cosmologically, however, District 5 is pathological Order. Its evil does not come from anarchy but from classification, procedure, deadlines, maps, quotas, and perfect administrative responsibility without moral responsibility. Every region contributes. Every adult watches. Every contestant receives rules. Every game produces one survivor. Nobody is officially exempt. Every death is recorded and assigned a purpose.

The state has failed at the complicated work of governing, so it clings to what it can still administer: ritualized control over powerless people. Order has become detached from justice and now treats continued obedience as proof that the system deserves to continue.

This makes District 5 an important contrast to the Argent Refrain. Dream is the capacity to imagine that the existing order is not the only possible world. The Program survives by preventing that act of imagination: children cannot prepare for what they cannot name, adults cannot organize against what they cannot discuss, and contestants are told that only one future remains.

## Morrí and Corwin

During the player's Surrender, a crow appears repeatedly. It watches from classroom windows, abandoned roofs, utility poles, and trees within forbidden zones. It does not explain itself or offer obvious aid. It appears at moments when the player chooses mercy, resists the rules' intended meaning, or refuses to let the Program define survival for them.

Near the end of the third day, the player is about to be killed by someone personally important to them. The crow lands somewhere impossible and looks directly at the player. A path opens where no path should exist, and Corwin stands on the other side.

This is the first time the player sees Corwin. He pulls them out at the final possible moment. Corwin performs the rescue, but Morrí chooses whom he finds. The player is not selected because of bloodline, combat ability, or promised destiny. They become visible to the Argent Refrain because their choices preserve an inner freedom District 5 was designed to extinguish.

The collar crosses the threshold with them. Cut off from District 5's signal, it no longer detonates, but it remains locked around the player's neck until the story decides how it can be removed. The physical remnant makes escape incomplete: the player has left the island without yet escaping what the island taught them.

Back in District 5, the government loses the player's signal. Officials cannot acknowledge an impossible escape, so the broadcast declares the student dead and edits the record accordingly. The state would rather falsify one death than admit that another future was possible.

## Signs of the Island Before District 5

The island's earlier history should initially be conveyed through fragments rather than a definitive account:

- Older place-names visible beneath government paint.
- A sealed cultural center repurposed as an operations building.
- Houses and paths older than the facility's official history.
- Government maps labeling visibly inhabited land as formerly undeveloped.
- Personal and ceremonial objects left behind in buildings.
- Areas that staff avoid or omit from announcements despite having no obvious tactical importance.

These details establish erasure without yet deciding whether the former community was expelled, relocated, killed, escaped, or remains connected to the island in some way.

## Things That Might Be Explored

1. **The Last Relationship** — who is about to kill the player when Morrí and Corwin intervene, and what combination of love, betrayal, fear, or sacrifice brought them to that moment?
2. **The Silent Parents** — what did the player's family tell themselves while preserving the secret, and what do they believe after watching the government announce their child's death?
3. **The Exemplar** — who officially survived the player's game, and how does that person remember the contestant who vanished?
4. **The Collar** — can it be removed safely, does it retain recordings or tracking data, and what does Corwin learn by examining a mundane machine built as an instrument of pathological Order?
5. **The Erased Island** — what happened to the Indigenous community, what survived the conversion of the island, and why are some parts of District 5 absent from official maps?
6. **The Failed Nation** — what precise political failure ended America's unipolar era, and did the National Surrender Program arise as an emergency measure, a calculated seizure of control, or an older practice given a new justification?
7. **A Door Left Open** — was the rescue a single impossible event, or has Morrí shown that District 5 can now be reached from other shadows?
8. **The Refusal to Watch** — what happens when an adult community finally chooses, together, to turn off the broadcast?

## First Adventure Notes

The first adventure begins with ordinary graduation and the relationships the player believes will define their adult life. The selection, drugged transit, and classroom revelation then turn those relationships into the human terrain of the three-day game.

The contestant roster and exact sequence of betrayals should be built only after the player character exists. Roughly thirty classmates are present, but the episode should focus on a smaller circle of deeply connected students rather than treating every contestant as equally important. Morrí's appearances provide the sole hint that District 5 is part of a larger reality. Corwin and the escape remain hidden until the final moment.
